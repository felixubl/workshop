Abecedarian Distance

A word is abecedarian when its letters already run in alphabetical order — billowy, almost, aegilops. Nearly no word is. But nearly every word would be, if the alphabet ran in some other order: zebra needs only Z moved to the front and A to the back. So the question stops being yes or no and becomes how far — the fewest pairs of letters you would have to swap in the alphabet itself before the word comes out sorted.

    Every one of the 26! orderings has a number: its place in the list of them, sorted the way a dictionary sorts. Ordinary A–Z is 0. The conversion is the Lehmer code, and it runs both ways, so this number names that alphabet exactly and nothing else.

    What the number means

    Swaps, not shifts

    The distance counts swaps of any two letters in the alphabet — the Cayley distance, which is 26 minus the number of cycles the reordering falls into. The worst possible is 25.

    Counting swaps of neighbouring letters instead gives a different number (Kendall tau, worst case 325), and the two disagree loudly. Rotating the alphabet by one, BCD…ZA, scores the maximum 25 on the first and a mild 25 out of 325 on the second. One asks how tangled the alphabet is; the other how far it has been carried. Both are printed above.

    Words that no alphabet can sort

    anna has no distance at all. Under any single ordering every copy of a letter has to sit together, and A here leaves and comes back — a…n…n…a. No permutation of the alphabet can fix that, so the answer is not a large number but no number.

    The test is exactly that: a word can be sorted by some alphabet if and only if no letter appears in two separate blocks. tee and aabb are fine; abab and knowledge are not.

    Only the first appearances count

    Once a word passes that test, everything about it collapses to one short string: its distinct letters in order of first appearance. bookkeeper and boker pose the identical problem, and length, repeats and doubled letters stop mattering entirely.

    Which is also why the count of alphabets that sort a word depends on nothing but how many different letters it uses: one in k! of them, for a word of k distinct letters.

    Why the obvious method is wrong

    The tempting shortcut is to reorder the word's own letters among their own alphabetical places and count that. It overcharges, because the letters the word never uses are parking spaces.

    For a word needing B before C before A, shuffling in place gives BCADE — a three-way rotation, two swaps. Parking A on D's square instead gives DBCAE: one swap, and D was doing nothing anyway. The search here checks the parking spaces, and is checked against brute force on every word over a five- and six-letter alphabet.
